# Evaluating and Negotiating an Offer

**Prerequisites**: You should already have completed [Communicating Under Pressure](/learning-paths/interview-preparation/communicating-under-pressure).
**Leads to**: After this, you'll be ready for [Common Interview Mistakes for QA Candidates](/learning-paths/interview-preparation/common-interview-mistakes-for-qa-candidates).

An offer arriving is where the interview process itself ends and a genuinely different skill begins — reading what's actually being offered, and, if warranted, negotiating it professionally. This module is deliberately scoped to that specific moment: evaluating and responding to an individual-contributor offer, not the longer-horizon career and compensation strategy TestAtlas's planned Career and Leadership in QA path will cover separately (coming soon).

## Why This Matters

**A candidate who accepts or declines reflexively.** An offer arrives, and a candidate, relieved simply to have one, accepts immediately without reading past the base salary number — missing that the total compensation includes a bonus structure with an unusual vesting schedule, and missing entirely that the role's actual scope, described casually in the offer conversation, is narrower than what was discussed throughout the interview process.

**A candidate who evaluates before responding.** A different candidate, receiving the identical offer, takes a day to review it fully — the base salary, bonus structure and vesting, benefits, and the actual role scope as described in writing — and identifies the same scope narrowing the first candidate missed. They raise it directly and professionally in a follow-up conversation, asking for clarification before accepting, rather than discovering the mismatch after starting.

Both candidates received the same offer. Only one of them actually read and evaluated it before responding.

## Reading and Responding to an Offer Professionally

**Evaluate total compensation, not just the base number**: base salary, bonus structure and its actual vesting terms, benefits, and any equity component together — this module's opening scenario shows exactly why stopping at the headline number misses real, consequential detail.

**Confirm role scope matches what was actually discussed**: a written offer is the place to catch and clarify any drift between what was described throughout the interview process and what's formally being offered — before accepting, not after starting.

**Negotiate with specific, factual grounding, never an ultimatum**: a negotiation grounded in market data, a competing offer, or a specific, factual gap between the offer and the role's actual market rate reads as professional; vague or emotionally-framed requests do not.

## What the Interviewer Is Really Evaluating

At this stage, there's typically no interviewer actively evaluating you — but how you handle this moment still shapes the relationship you're starting. A hiring manager or recruiter is implicitly noting:

- **Whether you read the offer carefully**: do your questions reflect genuine understanding of what was offered
- **Professionalism in negotiation**: is any counter-request grounded in specifics, not vague dissatisfaction
- **Clarity about what actually matters to you**: can you articulate specifically what you're asking for and why

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Evaluating an offer by the base salary number alone, without reading the full compensation structure.**
This module's opening scenario's entire gap traces to exactly this — a real, consequential detail (bonus vesting) missed by stopping at the headline number.

**Mistake 2: Accepting without confirming the actual role scope matches what was discussed throughout the interview process.**
A written offer is the last, easiest point to catch and clarify this kind of drift — after starting, it becomes a much harder conversation.

**Mistake 3: Negotiating with a vague or emotionally-framed request instead of specific, factual grounding.**
"I feel like I deserve more" is far weaker than "based on [specific market data or a competing offer], I'd like to discuss the base salary specifically."

## Best Practices

**Practice 1: Read the full offer — base, bonus and its vesting, benefits, equity if applicable — before responding at all.**
This is the single practice that would have caught this module's opening scenario's real, missed detail.

**Practice 2: Explicitly confirm the offered role's scope matches what was discussed throughout the process, in writing, before accepting.**
This is far easier to clarify before you start than after.

**Practice 3: Ground any negotiation in specific, factual evidence — market data, a competing offer, or a clear gap — never an ultimatum or vague dissatisfaction.**
This is what makes a negotiation request professional and credible, rather than adversarial.

:::note From the Field
A candidate, reviewing a written offer carefully, noticed the on-call rotation described was more frequent than what had been mentioned casually during the interview loop. Rather than accepting and discovering this later, they asked directly: "the offer describes on-call as every third week — earlier conversations mentioned roughly every sixth. Can you help me understand the discrepancy?" The clarification revealed a genuine miscommunication on the hiring team's side, corrected before the candidate accepted — a mismatch that would have caused real frustration if only discovered after starting.
:::

:::tip Senior QA Insight
A newer candidate treats receiving an offer as the finish line, to be accepted quickly out of relief. A senior candidate treats it as the last remaining checkpoint to actually verify — reading it fully, confirming scope, and negotiating professionally if warranted — because it's far easier to raise a concern before accepting than after starting.
:::

- Evaluate total compensation — base, bonus and its vesting, benefits, equity — not just the headline salary number.
- Confirm the offered role's scope matches what was actually discussed throughout the interview process, before accepting.
- Ground any negotiation in specific, factual evidence, never a vague or emotionally-framed request.
- A written offer is the easiest, last point to catch and clarify a mismatch — before starting, not after.
