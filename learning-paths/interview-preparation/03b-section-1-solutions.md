# Section 1 Solutions

These are the answers to the [Section 1 Review](/learning-paths/interview-preparation/section-1-review)'s Knowledge Check. If you haven't attempted the five scenarios yet, do that first.

## Scenario 1: The Underspecified Prompt

**Correct answer**: This is a scenario/take-home-style round, per [How QA Interviews Are Structured](/learning-paths/interview-preparation/how-qa-interviews-are-structured). Before answering, ask a clarifying question — for example, what platform, and whether payment processing itself is in scope or only the UI flow.

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — an open-ended prompt evaluates structured reasoning, and asking a clarifying question is the first, necessary step, not a delay.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Immediately listing testing types without clarifying scope is exactly the mistake the module's opening scenario describes.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether round recognition and the clarifying-question habit were understood as the actual first move, not optional.

## Scenario 2: The Number That Doesn't Hold Up

**Correct answer**: A specific, retrievable example behind the claim is missing — per [Presenting Your Testing Work Credibly](/learning-paths/interview-preparation/presenting-your-testing-work-credibly), the candidate should have prepared two or three fully fleshed-out examples behind each resume line before the interview, not just the summary number.

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — a real number with no specific memory or example behind it collapses under any probing.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Simply restating the number more confidently doesn't solve the actual problem — the missing piece is a concrete example, not more emphasis.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "prepare specific examples before the interview" was understood as the actual fix, not just "know your resume better."

## Scenario 3: The Unstructured Story

**Correct answer**: Apply the STAR structure explicitly, and specifically ensure the Action section — what they actually did — gets the majority of the answer, per [Behavioral Interviews: The STAR Method for QA](/learning-paths/interview-preparation/behavioral-interviews-the-star-method-for-qa).

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — a real story told without structure buries the exact information (the candidate's specific actions) an interviewer needs.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Telling the story with more enthusiasm or detail, without restructuring it, doesn't fix the actual problem — structure, not volume, is what's missing.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether STAR's specific purpose (making useful information findable) was understood, not just memorized as an acronym.

## Scenario 4: Too Much Background

**Correct answer**: This violates STAR's weighting guidance — Situation and Task should be brief (one or two sentences each), leaving the majority of the answer for Action.

**Explanation**: This is the module's own central lesson about STAR's uneven weighting, not equal parts for each of the four letters.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Assuming STAR means spending roughly equal time on each section is exactly Mistake 2 the module names directly.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether the specific, uneven weighting (Action as the majority) was understood, not just STAR's four labels.

## Scenario 5: A Real Story, Poorly Told

**Correct answer**: The story was very likely told without STAR's structure — a genuinely strong example can still read as "hard to follow" if it's delivered chronologically and unstructured rather than organized into brief Situation/Task followed by a detailed Action and a specific Result.

**Explanation**: This connects Section 1's own central point directly — content and structure are separate properties, and a strong story with weak structure still fails to land.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Assuming the story itself was the problem, rather than its delivery, misses the module's actual lesson.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "structure matters independently of content quality" was understood as this section's throughline, not just a fact about one module.
