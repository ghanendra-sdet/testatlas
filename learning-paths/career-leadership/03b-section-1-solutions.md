# Section 1 Solutions

These are the answers to the [Section 1 Review](/learning-paths/career-leadership/section-1-review)'s Knowledge Check. If you haven't attempted the five scenarios yet, do that first.

## Scenario 1: The Ambiguous Promotion

**Correct answer**: Before accepting, they should have asked specific questions — the actual weekly split of technical versus people work, whether the role includes direct reports, and how success is measured in six months — per [QA Career Roadmap: IC vs. Technical Lead vs. Manager](/learning-paths/career-leadership/qa-career-roadmap-ic-vs-technical-lead-vs-manager).

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — accepting a title change without confirming what it actually involves risks ending up in a genuinely different job than expected.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Assuming "QA Lead" has a consistent, predictable meaning across organizations is exactly the mistake the module warns against — the title alone doesn't reliably indicate the track.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether the specific clarifying questions were understood as the actual first step, not just "think it over."

## Scenario 2: The Overruled Lead

**Correct answer**: Technical credibility built *before* the title, not the title itself, per [Developing Leadership Skills and Technical Credibility](/learning-paths/career-leadership/developing-leadership-skills-and-technical-credibility) — the room hasn't seen this person's judgment demonstrated on anything difficult yet.

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — a title alone doesn't grant the credibility it's meant to reflect.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Assuming the developers were simply being difficult or political misses the module's actual point — credibility has to be earned through visible, repeated demonstration, not assumed from a title.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "credibility must be built before the title, not assumed from it" was understood as the core lesson, not just a fact about one meeting.

## Scenario 3: The Unshared Fix

**Correct answer**: A personal-branding gap — genuinely valuable work that stayed invisible outside their own team, per [Personal Branding for Test Engineers](/learning-paths/career-leadership/personal-branding-for-test-engineers).

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — excellent work that nobody outside the immediate team learns about builds no reputation, no matter how good it is.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Concluding the work itself wasn't valuable enough misses the actual lesson — the problem was visibility, not the quality or difficulty of the work.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "strong work must be made visible beyond your team to build reputation" was understood as the takeaway, not just "work harder."

## Scenario 4: The Generic Post

**Correct answer**: A specific, real example is missing — the substance-first principle says only work genuinely useful to someone else facing a similar problem is worth sharing, and generic advice with no real case behind it doesn't clear that bar.

**Explanation**: This directly restates the module's substance-first principle — content without a specific problem attached reads as filler.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Concluding the engineer simply needs to promote the post more misses the actual gap — the content itself, not its distribution, is the problem.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether the substance-first principle was understood as a content requirement, not a distribution or marketing problem.

## Scenario 5: The IC Who Feels Like a Fallback

**Correct answer**: This section explicitly rejects that assumption — the Individual Contributor track (Staff/Principal QA Engineer, QA Architect) is a legitimate, equally senior destination, not a consolation prize for not becoming a manager.

**Explanation**: This connects directly to [QA Career Roadmap](/learning-paths/career-leadership/qa-career-roadmap-ic-vs-technical-lead-vs-manager)'s core point — none of the three tracks is a "higher" version of another.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Accepting the "less senior" framing as accurate is exactly Mistake 3 from that module, treating the IC track as a fallback rather than an equally valid choice based on genuine fit.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "the three tracks are different jobs, not a hierarchy" was understood as this section's throughline, not just a fact about one module.

## Section 1 Complete

Across three modules, this section established the foundation every later section builds on: recognizing which career track is actually on offer, building the technical credibility every track depends on, and making genuinely valuable work visible beyond your own team.
