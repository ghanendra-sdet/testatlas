# Section 3 Solutions

These are the answers to the [Section 3 Review](/learning-paths/career-leadership/section-3-review)'s Knowledge Check. If you haven't attempted the five scenarios yet, do that first.

## Scenario 1: The Unaddressed Process Gap

**Correct answer**: Propose a small, provable fix and pilot it on their own work first, then present evidence framed around a shared goal, per [Leading Without Authority](/learning-paths/career-leadership/leading-without-authority) — not wait for a formal title.

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — most real influence happens without formal authority, and waiting for a title delays solving a visible, fixable problem for no real benefit.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Escalating the concern loudly without any evidence or small-scale proof is far less persuasive than a demonstrated, small fix.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether the specific mechanism (small proof, evidence, shared-goal framing) was understood as the actual path to change, not just "speak up more."

## Scenario 2: The Inconsistent Team

**Correct answer**: Explicit, deliberate structure — a stated work-allocation method and purposeful rituals, per [Running QA Teams](/learning-paths/career-leadership/running-qa-teams).

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — ad hoc, case-by-case decisions feel flexible but compound into inconsistency and imbalanced workload over time.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Assuming the team simply needs more meetings or more oversight misses the actual gap — what's missing is explicit, consistent structure, not more managerial attention.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "explicit structure prevents compounding inconsistency" was understood as the core lesson, not just "the Lead needs to try harder."

## Scenario 3: The Repeated Question

**Correct answer**: Switch from giving direct answers to guided questioning — asking what the junior tester thinks and why, per [Mentoring Engineers](/learning-paths/career-leadership/mentoring-engineers).

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — direct answers, even correct ones, don't build the underlying reasoning process, so the same type of question resurfaces.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Simply explaining the answer again, more clearly or in more detail, doesn't address the actual gap — the issue is process, not clarity of explanation.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether "mentoring should build judgment, not just transfer answers" was understood as the fix, not just "explain better."

## Scenario 4: The Centralized Bottleneck

**Correct answer**: The cost is a bottleneck and stalled team growth, per [Delegation and Decision Making](/learning-paths/career-leadership/delegation-and-decision-making) — routine, low-risk decisions should be delegated, reserving personal review for decisions with major, hard-to-reverse consequences.

**Explanation**: This connects directly to that module's delegate-versus-keep framework — over-centralizing decisions with small, recoverable consequences produces exactly this cost.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Concluding the Lead simply needs to review faster misses the actual fix — the issue is what's being centralized, not the speed of review.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether the consequence-based delegation framework was understood as the fix, not just "the Lead is a bottleneck, so work faster."

## Scenario 5: The Side-Picking Resolution

**Correct answer**: The Lead resolved the conflict at the position level rather than the interest level, per [Conflict Resolution](/learning-paths/career-leadership/conflict-resolution) — the underlying interests behind each position were never surfaced or addressed.

**Explanation**: This is the module's own opening scenario restated — picking a side settles immediate friction but leaves the underlying disagreement to resurface in a new form.

**Alternative approaches considered**: Assuming a stricter or faster decision process would fix this misses the actual gap — the problem is that interests were never surfaced, not that the decision was slow.

**Real-world reasoning**: Tests whether the positions-versus-interests distinction was understood as the actual mechanism, not just "make a firmer decision next time."

## Section 3 Complete
