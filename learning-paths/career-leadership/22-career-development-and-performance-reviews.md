# Career Development and Performance Reviews

**Prerequisites**: [Hiring and Interviewing Test Engineers](/learning-paths/career-leadership/hiring-and-interviewing-test-engineers)
**Leads to**: After this, you'll be ready for [Giving Feedback](/learning-paths/career-leadership/giving-feedback).

## Why This Matters

**A QA Manager who treats performance reviews as an annual formality.** A QA Manager conducts performance reviews once a year, largely reconstructed from memory in the days before the review, using generic language ("meets expectations," "strong team player") that could apply to almost anyone on the team. The engineer receiving it learns little they didn't already know, the review has no real connection to specific growth goals discussed months earlier (if any were), and it functions mostly as a compliance exercise neither party finds genuinely useful.

**A QA Manager who treats performance reviews as one checkpoint in an ongoing process.** A peer instead keeps a running, specific record of notable work and growth throughout the year, has regular, shorter check-ins connecting current work to each person's stated development goals, and uses the formal review to synthesize that ongoing record rather than reconstruct it from scratch. The review contains specific, memorable examples and directly ties back to goals set months earlier — because it's the culmination of continuous attention, not an isolated annual event.

Both managers conducted the required review. Only one made it genuinely useful — because a performance review's value depends entirely on whether it reflects real, specific, ongoing attention to someone's growth, not on the formal document itself.

## Career Development Is Individual, Not a Template

A generic career-development template — the same growth areas and next steps applied to every engineer on a team — misses the actual point, since people at similar levels can have genuinely different strengths, interests, and gaps. Effective career development starts from the specific person: what do they want (a question worth asking directly, not assuming, since not everyone wants the same track — see [QA Career Roadmap](/learning-paths/career-leadership/qa-career-roadmap-ic-vs-technical-lead-vs-manager)), what's genuinely holding them back from their next step, and what specific, concrete opportunities would address that gap.

## Making Performance Reviews Genuinely Useful

- **Keep a running record throughout the year, not just before the review.** A brief note after a notable piece of work — what happened, what it demonstrated — makes the eventual review specific and evidence-based rather than reconstructed from vague memory.
- **Connect the review explicitly to previously stated goals.** If a growth goal was discussed six months earlier, the review should explicitly reference it — did the person make progress, what got in the way, what's the next step — rather than starting the goal conversation fresh each cycle.
- **Use specific examples, not generic descriptors.** "Handled the Q3 incident investigation independently, correctly identifying a root cause across three services" is far more useful, and more credible, than "shows strong technical skills."
- **Treat the formal review as a synthesis point, not the only development conversation of the year.** Regular, shorter check-ins throughout the year, connected to stated goals, do most of the actual developmental work — the formal review consolidates and formalizes it.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Reconstructing a performance review from memory in the days before it's due.**
This module's opening scenario — a review based on recent, vaguely remembered impressions rather than a genuine, specific record misses most of a year's actual work and growth.

**Mistake 2: Using generic language that could describe almost anyone on the team.**
Vague descriptors like "strong team player" provide no specific, actionable information — specificity is what makes a review actually useful for growth.

**Mistake 3: Applying the same generic career-development plan to every engineer at a similar level.**
Different people have different strengths, interests, and gaps — a one-size-fits-all plan misses what would actually help this specific person.

**Mistake 4: Treating the review as disconnected from goals discussed earlier in the year.**
A review that doesn't reference previously stated development goals loses the continuity that makes career development feel like genuine progress rather than a series of unconnected annual conversations.

## Best Practices

**Practice 1: Keep a brief, ongoing record of notable work throughout the year.**
A short note after significant work — a specific example of skill demonstrated, a growth area observed — builds toward a specific, evidence-based review rather than one reconstructed from memory.

**Practice 2: Ask directly what someone wants from their career, rather than assuming.**
Different people genuinely want different things — the three tracks from [QA Career Roadmap](/learning-paths/career-leadership/qa-career-roadmap-ic-vs-technical-lead-vs-manager) mean development plans should be built around a person's actual stated goals, not an assumed default path.

**Practice 3: Use specific, memorable examples throughout the review, not generic descriptors.**
Concrete examples are both more useful for the recipient and more credible — they show the review reflects real observation, not a template filled in with plausible-sounding language.

**Practice 4: Explicitly reference and follow up on goals set in previous reviews or check-ins.**
This continuity is what turns a series of annual documents into an actual, connected development trajectory the person can see progress on.

:::note From the Field
A QA Manager at AtlasBank had, for several years, written performance reviews in the week before they were due, relying on general impressions and producing largely generic feedback. After noticing two strong engineers leave within the same quarter, partly citing a lack of clear growth direction, the manager began keeping a simple running document — a few sentences after any notable piece of work throughout the year, plus quarterly, informal fifteen-minute check-ins tied to each person's own stated goals. The following year's reviews were noticeably more specific and were, based on direct feedback from the team, received as far more useful and credible — not because the manager suddenly became a better writer, but because the review finally reflected genuine, ongoing attention rather than an annual reconstruction from memory.
:::

## Interview Questions

**Q1: How do you make performance reviews genuinely useful rather than just an annual formality?**

*What to look for*: A description of ongoing attention (a running record, regular check-ins) rather than a plan to simply write a more thoughtful document once a year — the strongest answers show the review is a synthesis point, not a standalone event.

**Q2: How do you build a career development plan for someone on your team?**

*What to look for*: A process that starts by asking what the person actually wants, not applying a generic template — showing awareness that different people have genuinely different goals.

:::note Common Interview Mistake
Many candidates describe performance reviews purely in terms of the document's structure or format, without discussing what happens throughout the rest of the year. A strong answer emphasizes ongoing attention and specific tracking as what actually makes the review valuable, not the review-writing process itself.
:::

**Q3: Tell me about a time you helped someone on your team achieve a specific career goal.**

*What to look for*: A real, specific example connecting a stated goal to concrete support and eventual progress — not a generic description of "supporting my team's growth" without a specific case.

## Glossary

**Career Development Plan**: A growth plan built around a specific individual's actual stated career goals, rather than a generic template applied uniformly across a team.

**Running Performance Record**: An ongoing, brief record of notable work and growth kept throughout the year, used to make formal performance reviews specific and evidence-based rather than reconstructed from memory.
